# Ethical Reflection

This automation touches something as personal as someone's mental state, so it's worth being honest about where it can fail and what that failure actually costs.

**When it's wrong.** The AI response is generated from five short data points - three numbers, a dropdown, and an optional note. That's a thin signal. The model can misread tone (e.g. read a flat "fine" note as low-risk when it isn't) or give a generically upbeat response on a day that actually needed something more careful. Because the message is short and low-stakes-sounding by design, a wrong response is unlikely to actively harm someone, but it can miss a moment where more care was warranted - the failure mode here is being unhelpfully generic, not giving dangerous advice.

**When it's late or down.** If the schedule trigger doesn't fire, or an email fails to send, the user simply doesn't get their daily prompt that day. There's no monitoring or fallback if the workflow silently breaks - a real production version would need alerting so a broken pipeline doesn't just quietly stop supporting someone who's come to rely on it, without either the user or the maintainer knowing.

**Would I trust it for my own health?** As a daily nudge and light mood log, yes - it's low-stakes and the worst outcome is a message that doesn't quite land. I would not trust it, and don't intend it, to be a safety net or a stand-in for professional care. The IF-branch that appends crisis resources on very low mood or very high stress exists precisely because I don't trust the AI response alone to be adequate in that situation - it's a fixed, human-written fallback, not something the model is asked to reason about.

**Where automation should stop and a human takes over.** The moment the numbers suggest someone might be in real distress, the tool's job is to hand off, not to help further. It should never try to be the support itself in that scenario - it should say, clearly and immediately, "call this, talk to this person," and stop. That's the line I built into the workflow: automation for the everyday check-in, but a hard-coded redirect to real human/professional support the moment the signal crosses a threshold. I'd extend this further in a real version - e.g. by letting the user name a trusted contact who gets notified after several low days in a row - but that also introduces consent and privacy questions (should a friend be pinged without the user choosing that in advance?) that I didn't have time to design carefully for this hackathon, so I left it out rather than build it half-considered.
